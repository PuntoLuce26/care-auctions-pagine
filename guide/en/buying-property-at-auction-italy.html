Buying property at auction in Italy

Judicial property auctions are a real way to buy a home in Italy: a public procedure, a court, a starting price and clear rules. This guide explains how it works, what it really costs and what to check before you bid.

What a judicial auction is

When a property is subject to enforcement, the court sells it on behalf of the debtor. The sale is public and runs through the Italian public sales portal (pvp.giustizia.it), where the sale notice, the court expert's appraisal and the case documents are published. No agent mediates: the buyer takes part in the procedure directly.

The four steps

  • Documents. Read the sale notice and the appraisal: they contain everything — starting price, minimum bid, condition, occupancy, costs and irregularities.
  • Viewing. Access is arranged with the custodian through the sales institute. A viewing is worth more than ten photographs.
  • Bid. Sealed bid with a deposit (usually 10% of the offer), following the sale notice.
  • Balance and transfer decree. After the award you pay the balance within the deadline and receive the transfer decree, which is your title of ownership.

What it really costs

The starting price is not the final cost. Add: the balance, taxes (2% or 9% depending on the purchase), transfer costs, any overdue condominium charges stated in the notice, remediation of irregularities, and renovation. The practical rule is to calculate a maximum offer that includes everything and never exceed it.

The number that matters

In the Italian municipalities where a comparison is possible, the gap between the court starting price and the estimated market value is around 43% median (OMI source, coverage declared). It is not an automatic discount: it is a margin that must be verified property by property.

The risks to check

  • Occupancy. An occupied property means time and cost to vacate: the sale notice always states whether it is vacant.
  • Irregularities. Building and cadastral irregularities are the buyer's responsibility: the appraisal lists them, and they must be read before bidding.
  • Condominium charges. Arrears can be significant: check them with the administrator or in the notice.
  • Timeline. From deposit to decree, months can pass: plan on real timelines, not ideal ones.
